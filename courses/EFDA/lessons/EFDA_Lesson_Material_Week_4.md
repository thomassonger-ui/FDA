## The Highest-Volume Preventive Procedures You'll Perform

If you work in a general practice, sealants, fluoride, and coronal polishing are the three procedures you'll perform most often as an EFDA. They are also three of the easiest to do incorrectly without anyone noticing — until the protective benefit disappears within months.

This week ties together caries science (Ch 15) with the hands-on prevention techniques in Ch 58 (polishing) and Ch 59 (sealants). Fluoride doesn't get its own chapter in Bird & Robinson — it's interwoven through caries and preventive content.

## Caries Risk Assessment: Why Prevention Targets the Right Patients

Caries is a multifactorial bacterial disease. Three conditions must coexist:

1. A susceptible tooth surface (enamel pits, fissures, root surfaces exposed by recession)
2. Cariogenic bacteria (primarily *Streptococcus mutans*, *Lactobacillus*)
3. Fermentable substrate (sugars and refined carbohydrates) over time

The intensity of the third factor — frequency and duration of sugar exposure — has more impact on caries risk than total quantity. A patient who sips one sugary coffee for three hours is at much higher risk than one who drinks four sodas in 20 minutes.

### High-risk indicators

- Multiple active carious lesions (existing decay)
- Restorations placed in the last 36 months
- Visible plaque on smooth surfaces
- Dry mouth (medication-induced, radiation-induced, Sjögren's)
- Frequent snacking on fermentable carbohydrates
- Soda/juice consumption between meals
- Inadequate fluoride exposure (no fluoridated water, infrequent toothpaste use)
- History of poor oral hygiene
- Orthodontic appliances

The EFDA-scope preventive procedures (sealants, fluoride, polishing) are most effective when targeted at higher-risk patients. Low-risk patients still benefit but should not receive unnecessary intervention.

## EFDA101 · Dental Sealants

### What sealants do

A pit-and-fissure sealant is a resin or glass ionomer material flowed into the deep occlusal grooves of a tooth and cured in place. The sealant physically blocks food and bacteria from entering the fissure, eliminating one of the primary caries-prone surfaces.

Properly placed sealants reduce occlusal caries by 60–80% over a 4-year period.

### Indications

- Newly erupted permanent molars (typically ages 6 and 12) in moderate-to-high caries risk patients
- Primary molars in high-risk children
- Deep pit-and-fissure anatomy on any tooth
- Early non-cavitated occlusal lesions (some preventive resin restoration cases)

### Contraindications

- Tooth that's fully restored on the occlusal surface (no fissure to seal)
- Existing cavitated occlusal caries — needs a restoration, not a sealant
- Patient unable to maintain isolation (very young, gagging, behavioral issues)

### Materials

| Type | Properties |
|---|---|
| **Resin-based filled** | Stronger, more wear-resistant; light-cure |
| **Resin-based unfilled (flowable)** | Better penetration into narrow fissures; light-cure |
| **Glass ionomer (GIC)** | Releases fluoride; less retention than resin; useful when isolation is poor |

### Four-step etch-rinse-place-cure protocol

1. **Clean** the occlusal surface — pumice prophy (no fluoride paste — it interferes with bonding); rinse and dry
2. **Isolate** — cotton rolls minimum, dental dam preferred. Any saliva contamination during the next step ruins the bond.
3. **Etch** — 35% phosphoric acid gel applied to all pits and fissures for **15 seconds** (manufacturer-specific). Surface should appear chalky white after rinsing.
4. **Rinse** thoroughly for 10–15 seconds. Dry with oil-free air for 10 seconds. Surface must be visibly dry; if not, re-isolate and re-dry.
5. **Place** sealant material with the manufacturer's applicator tip, flowing into the fissure depth. Avoid bubbles.
6. **Cure** with the light per manufacturer time (typically 20–30 seconds per surface).
7. **Inspect** — check for voids, marginal gaps, retention. Use explorer to verify the sealant is fused to the enamel.
8. **Occlusion** — check with articulating paper. Adjust if high.

### Common failures

- **Sealant lost within months** — moisture contamination during etch or place step. Re-isolate next time.
- **Air bubbles in the sealant** — applicator tip moved too fast or wasn't held perpendicular to the fissure.
- **Etch pattern not visible** — etch time too short, or tooth wasn't cleaned. Re-clean and re-etch.

## EFDA102 · Fluoride Placement

### How fluoride works

Fluoride reduces caries through three mechanisms:

- **Remineralization** — fluoride ions incorporate into demineralized enamel as fluorapatite, which is more acid-resistant than the native hydroxyapatite
- **Reduced acid production** — fluoride inhibits the bacterial enzymes that ferment sugars into acid
- **Inhibits enamel demineralization** — fluoride present in saliva at low concentrations slows the loss of mineral when pH drops

The clinical effect is dose-dependent: higher concentration plus longer contact time equals greater benefit. But systemic fluoride exposure also carries risk (fluorosis in developing teeth, acute toxicity at very high doses).

### Delivery vehicles

| Vehicle | Concentration | Use |
|---|---|---|
| **Foam** | 1.23% APF or 2% NaF | In-office, in fluoride trays; 4-minute contact |
| **Gel** | 1.23% APF or 2% NaF | In-office, in trays; 4-minute contact |
| **Varnish** | 5% NaF | Brushed onto teeth; sets quickly; stays in contact for hours |
| **Toothpaste** | 0.05–0.15% NaF | At-home daily use |
| **Mouth rinse** | 0.05% NaF (daily) or 0.2% (weekly) | At-home use |

### When to use each

- **Foam or gel in trays** — recurring patient (twice-yearly recall) with moderate caries risk
- **Varnish** — high-risk patient, pediatric patient, patient who can't tolerate trays, post-scaling sensitivity treatment
- **Toothpaste / rinse** — daily at-home prevention (not EFDA scope; patient education only)

### Application protocol (foam/gel in trays)

1. Patient seated upright
2. Tooth surfaces cleaned with pumice prophy and rinsed
3. Lips and tongue protected; saliva ejector positioned
4. Trays loaded — sufficient material to cover all teeth but NOT overflow (aspiration / swallowing risk)
5. Trays seated for the manufacturer's recommended time (usually 4 minutes)
6. Patient bites lightly; do not let them swallow
7. After time, remove trays, evacuate excess; advise patient to spit, not rinse
8. No food or drink for 30 minutes

### Varnish protocol

1. Surfaces cleaned of plaque (no prophy paste needed)
2. Lightly dry teeth (does not need to be perfectly dry — varnish tolerates moisture)
3. Brush varnish onto all tooth surfaces using the supplied applicator
4. Tell patient no brushing or hard chewing tonight; varnish sets in saliva over the next several hours

### Safety

- Acute fluoride toxicity threshold is ~5 mg/kg body weight — small children at higher risk
- Never let a young child swallow excess fluoride
- Patients with severe gag reflex — use varnish instead of trays
- Patients sensitive to fluoride or with known allergy — avoid; document

## EFDA103 · Coronal Polishing

### What it actually does

Coronal polishing removes extrinsic stains, plaque, and pellicle from the clinical crowns of teeth using a rubber cup or brush and a polishing paste. It does NOT remove calculus — that requires scaling (a hygienist or dentist procedure, outside EFDA scope).

### Selective polishing

The modern standard is **selective polishing**: polish only those surfaces with visible extrinsic stain or plaque accumulation, not all surfaces routinely. Reasons:

- Routine polishing of all surfaces removes 5–10 μm of fluoride-rich outer enamel per session
- This thin outer layer is the most caries-resistant part of the tooth
- Polishing unnecessarily strips this protection without clinical benefit
- For most patients, scaling + selective polishing is the appropriate finish

### Instruments

- **Slow-speed handpiece** with prophy angle attachment
- **Rubber cup** (soft, medium, or firm) — for facial, lingual, buccal surfaces
- **Bristle brush** (small or large) — for occlusal pits and fissures
- **Prophy paste** in three grit grades:
  - Coarse: heavy stains (use sparingly; most abrasive)
  - Medium: moderate stains (standard for most cases)
  - Fine: minimal stains, polishing pass after coarser grit
- **Saliva ejector** and HVE if working with significant water spray
- **Floss** — to remove paste from interproximal areas at the end

### Technique

1. Cleaned operatory; patient draped
2. Apply paste to rubber cup
3. Set handpiece to slow speed (~3,000 rpm or less)
4. Adapt rubber cup to tooth surface — flare the edge slightly under the gingival margin
5. Move in overlapping strokes, light intermittent pressure — NEVER continuous heavy pressure
6. Polish one tooth at a time (or two adjacent), then re-apply paste
7. Rinse and evacuate frequently to prevent paste accumulation
8. Floss interproximal areas at the end to remove paste residue

### Air polishing

Air polishing (air + water + abrasive powder, usually sodium bicarbonate or glycine) is an alternative for heavy stain removal. Requires specialized equipment. EFDA programs generally cover rubber-cup polishing; air polishing may or may not be in scope depending on training and state rules — confirm with your supervising dentist.

### Contraindications

- Patients with respiratory conditions (asthma, COPD) — risk from aerosols
- Patients on sodium-restricted diets — concern for air polishing with sodium bicarbonate
- Exposed cementum or root surfaces — too abrasive; can damage softer cementum
- Newly erupted teeth or hypoplastic enamel — fragile surface
- Areas around composite restorations — paste can dull the surface; use a fine paste

## Key Takeaways

1. **Caries is multifactorial.** Susceptible surface + bacteria + fermentable substrate over time. Target preventive procedures at the patients who have all three.
2. **Sealants reduce occlusal caries by 60–80%** over 4 years — but only when placed correctly. Isolation is the make-or-break step.
3. **Etch makes the surface look chalky white.** If it doesn't, re-clean and re-etch.
4. **Match the fluoride vehicle to the patient.** Tray foam for compliant adults, varnish for kids / high-risk / sensitive patients.
5. **Selective polishing is the modern standard.** Polish what's stained, not every surface.
6. **Polish at slow speed with light intermittent pressure.** Continuous heavy pressure damages enamel and irritates pulp.
7. **Floss at the end.** Paste left interproximally is a foreign body.
8. **Patient education is part of every preventive procedure.** Teach the at-home routine that complements your in-office work.
