## Why Field Preparation Matters

Every restorative procedure you assist with depends on one thing: a clean, dry, accessible operative field. Moisture contaminates bonding chemistry. Tissue encroaches on margins. Adjacent teeth interfere with placement. Three EFDA-scope procedures protect the field: dental dam isolation (EFDA111), retraction cord placement (EFDA109), and matrix system placement (EFDA106). Master these three and the dentist's restorative work goes faster, comes out better, and lasts longer.

---

## Moisture Control: The Principle Behind the Procedures

Restorative materials — composites, sealants, cements, bonding agents — are chemistry-sensitive. Most modern adhesive systems require an absolutely dry surface to form a durable bond. Saliva, blood, and crevicular fluid contain enzymes and proteins that interfere with resin polymerization and dentin bonding.

Three failure modes traced directly to poor moisture control:

- **Premature failure of composite restorations** — saliva contamination during etch or bond steps weakens micromechanical retention; the restoration debonds within months
- **Sealant loss** — moisture during etch destroys the resin tags before they cure into the enamel
- **Inflamed margins on crowns** — saliva contamination of cement compromises seal at the margin, leading to recurrent caries and gingival inflammation

Moisture control happens at three escalating levels: cotton roll isolation (simple cases), saliva ejector + cotton rolls (most short procedures), and dental dam isolation (anything where the field must be absolutely dry).

## EFDA111 · Dental Dam Placement

### Why dental dam

A properly placed dental dam delivers four benefits simultaneously: it isolates the tooth from saliva, protects the patient from aspirating instruments or materials, retracts soft tissue out of the operative field, and increases visibility for the operator. It also reduces aerosol generation — a meaningful infection-control benefit you carry over from Week 1.

### Armamentarium

- **Dental dam material** — latex or non-latex (nitrile/silicone). Thickness: thin (light), medium (preferred for most), heavy (when extra retraction is needed). Color: dark, contrasting (green or blue) — easier on the eyes than light.
- **Dental dam frame** — Young frame (metal U-shape) or Nygard-Østby (plastic, radiolucent). Holds the dam taut, exposes the field.
- **Dental dam punch** — creates the tooth holes; multiple hole sizes match the size of the tooth being isolated.
- **Dental dam clamp** — anchors the dam to the most posterior tooth in the field. Pick a clamp by tooth: winged vs. wingless, anterior vs. posterior, with or without retentive points for retracted gingiva.
- **Dental dam forceps** — places and removes the clamp without slipping.
- **Floss / dental tape** — pulls the dam between contacts; tied to the clamp bow as a safety tether.
- **Stamp or template** — pre-marks the hole positions on the dam material.

### Hole spacing

The standard "ideal arch" template stamps holes corresponding to tooth positions in the maxillary and mandibular arches. Holes spaced too close create gaps between teeth where saliva leaks through. Too far apart and the dam stretches and tears.

Standard hole sizes:
- Smallest (size 5): lower incisors
- Medium (size 3-4): premolars and cuspids
- Largest (size 1-2): molars and anchor teeth

### Placement sequence

1. Anesthetic if needed
2. Floss the contacts to confirm dam material will pass through
3. Punch holes per template
4. Place clamp on anchor tooth, tie safety floss to the bow
5. Stretch dam over the clamp bow
6. Pull the dam through each contact using floss
7. Invert the dam edge into the gingival sulcus around each isolated tooth — this is the seal
8. Place frame and adjust for taut, even tension

### Removal sequence

1. Cut the dam septa between each pair of holes with scissors
2. Remove the clamp first using forceps
3. Wipe excess saline from the gingiva
4. Inspect for any remaining dam fragments (count holes vs. fragments)

### Common errors and fixes

- **Dam tears at the hole edge** — hole was punched too close to another, or punch wasn't sharp; re-punch on a fresh location
- **Saliva leaks past the seal** — dam not inverted into the sulcus; pull edge gently with explorer to invert
- **Soft tissue trauma at the clamp** — clamp size wrong for tooth; reselect a clamp that seats without pinching gingiva

## EFDA109 · Retraction Cord Placement

### What retraction cord does

Retraction cord serves two purposes simultaneously: it physically pushes the free gingival margin away from the prepared tooth (so the dentist can see and access the margin), and it provides hemostasis (the cord is usually impregnated with a chemical agent that constricts the small vessels in the sulcus). Both effects are required before any final impression or final cementation in a fixed prosthodontic case.

### Cord types

| Type | Description | Use |
|---|---|---|
| **Plain cotton, knitted** | Soft, absorbent, no chemical | When patient is sensitive to chemicals; surgeon will achieve hemostasis differently |
| **Aluminum chloride impregnated** | Most common; mild hemostat, well-tolerated | Standard for most restorative cases |
| **Epinephrine impregnated** | Strong vasoconstrictor | Used selectively; avoid in patients with cardiovascular disease, hyperthyroidism, or known sensitivity |
| **Ferric sulfate / aluminum sulfate** | Strong hemostat | When bleeding is hard to control |

### Cord sizes

Sizes are numbered #000 (thinnest) through #3 (thickest). Choose by sulcus depth and gingival biotype:

- **#000 / #00** — thin biotype, shallow sulcus, anterior teeth
- **#0 / #1** — average biotype, most cases
- **#2 / #3** — thick biotype, deep sulcus, molars

### Single-cord vs. double-cord technique

- **Single cord** — place one cord deep into the sulcus, leave 5–10 minutes, remove and impress immediately. Works for routine cases.
- **Double cord (preferred for sub-gingival margins)** — place a thin (#000) cord deep first, then a thicker (#1) cord above it. Remove the top cord just before impression; the bottom cord stays during the impression and is removed afterward. Yields more reliable margin visibility on deep preparations.

### Placement steps

1. Isolate quadrant (cotton rolls or dental dam)
2. Cut a length of cord ~1.5 cm longer than the circumference of the tooth
3. Use a cord packer or thin instrument to gently work the cord into the sulcus, starting at the distal-buccal corner
4. Press circumferentially, not vertically — vertical pressure damages the junctional epithelium
5. Leave a small "tail" outside the sulcus for easy removal
6. Wait 5–10 minutes for hemostasis and mechanical retraction to work
7. To remove: pull along the long axis of the tooth, slowly; if bleeding restarts, repack briefly

### Safety considerations

- **Epinephrine cord risk** — absorbed through abraded gingiva. Avoid in patients with hypertension, hyperthyroidism, or cardiovascular disease. Document patient medical history before reaching for epinephrine cord.
- **Pressure-induced recession** — over-aggressive packing causes permanent gingival recession. Pack gently and along the long axis.
- **Cord left behind** — count cord pieces in and out. A retained cord fragment causes inflammation and abscess.

## EFDA106 · Matrix Systems

### Why matrices

A matrix is a temporary wall that recreates the missing proximal surface of a tooth so the dentist can pack restorative material against it. Without a matrix, the material extrudes into the embrasure (gap between teeth), creating overhangs, open contacts, and uncomfortable restorations.

A good matrix gives you:
- A tight contact with the adjacent tooth
- A correctly contoured proximal surface (not flat — anatomically curved)
- A secure gingival seal at the margin

### Two main matrix categories

**Tofflemire (universal) matrix**

The classic stainless-steel band with a retainer that wraps around the entire tooth. Best for full-quadrant or large restorations.

- Band sits in the retainer slot, screwed in place
- Band wraps the tooth like a collar
- Wedge slides between adjacent teeth at the gingival margin to seal and create contact pressure
- Best for: amalgam, large composite restorations, molars

**Sectional matrix system**

Pre-curved metal bands that wrap only the proximal surface, held by a separating ring. Better for single Class II composites.

- Band sits against the proximal wall of the tooth
- Separating ring tucks under the contact and pushes adjacent teeth slightly apart
- Wedge seals the gingival margin
- Best for: single Class II composites where tight contact matters

### Wedge selection

Wedges seal the gingival margin and stabilize the band. Three types:

- **Wooden** — anatomic, absorbs slightly, easy to compress; standard choice
- **Plastic** — translucent (light-cure friendly), reusable, often less anatomic
- **Special anatomic** — pre-curved for specific tooth shapes (molars vs. premolars)

Choose wedge size to fit the embrasure without forcing — too small leaves a gap, too large causes pain and trauma.

### Placement steps (Tofflemire example)

1. Select band size and shape (universal, MOD, anterior); thread into retainer
2. Tighten the retainer screw with the slot facing the gingival surface
3. Slide assembly over the tooth from the occlusal direction
4. Position so the band extends 1 mm above the marginal ridge and 1 mm below the gingival margin
5. Insert wedge from the larger embrasure (lingual or buccal), gingival to the band
6. Verify seal and contour with the explorer
7. Tighten the retainer further if needed for circumferential adaptation

### Common errors

- **Open contact after restoration** — band not pre-burnished against adjacent tooth; or wedge not seated tight enough
- **Gingival overhang** — wedge missing or too short; gingival edge of band not sealed
- **Flat proximal contour** — band not pre-burnished; use a burnisher to curve the band before placement

## How These Three Procedures Work Together

In a typical Class II composite restoration the dentist will ask you to:

1. **Place dental dam** for isolation and access
2. **(Sometimes) place retraction cord** if the margin extends sub-gingivally
3. **Place matrix + wedge** after preparation, before the dentist applies bonding agent
4. **Maintain visibility and dryness** while the dentist places and cures the restorative material
5. **Remove the matrix, wedge, and dam** in that order after the restoration is finished
6. **Floss the contact** to confirm a tight, healthy contact was created

You're orchestrating the dentist's field. Get it right and the restoration is fast, dry, and well-contoured. Get it wrong and the work has to be redone.

## Key Takeaways

1. **Moisture control is chemistry-driven.** Saliva contamination breaks bonding. Master the field, master the restoration.
2. **Dental dam isolates, protects, retracts, and improves visibility — all at once.** Invert the edge into the sulcus or the seal fails.
3. **Retraction cord choice has medical implications.** Know what's in your cord; ask about cardiovascular history before reaching for epinephrine.
4. **Single vs. double cord depends on margin location.** Sub-gingival margins → double cord, always.
5. **Tofflemire vs. sectional matrix is a contact-quality decision.** Sectional + separating ring = tighter contact for Class II composites.
6. **Wedge from the larger embrasure.** Lingual or buccal, whichever is wider for that tooth.
7. **Count cord pieces in and out.** A retained fragment is a real complication.
