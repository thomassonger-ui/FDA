## EFDA Scope of Practice in Florida

The Expanded Functions Dental Assistant (EFDA) is a credentialed clinical role recognized by the Florida Board of Dentistry under Chapter 466 of Florida Statutes and Rule 64B5 of the Florida Administrative Code. An EFDA may legally perform thirteen designated "remediable" or expanded-function tasks under the supervision of a Florida-licensed dentist.

### What you may do as an EFDA

- Place and remove dental dams, retraction cord, matrices, periodontal dressings, and temporary restorations
- Apply liners, bases, and bonding systems
- Take alginate impressions and pour study models
- Place pit-and-fissure sealants
- Apply topical fluoride
- Perform coronal polishing of clinical crowns
- Fabricate provisional/temporary crowns
- Remove sutures and excess dental cement

### What you may NOT do as an EFDA

- Diagnose dental disease
- Create or modify a treatment plan
- Prepare teeth for restorations
- Place final restorations
- Perform any independent practice of dentistry

### Levels of supervision

Florida distinguishes two relevant supervision levels for EFDAs:

- **Direct supervision** — the dentist is physically present in the office, has diagnosed the condition, authorized the procedure, and will evaluate the work before patient dismissal.
- **Indirect supervision** — the dentist has authorized the procedure and is available in the office, but is not required to be present in the operatory.

Different expanded functions require different supervision levels. As a rule of thumb, anything that contacts the prepared tooth structure or involves clinical decision-making requires direct supervision. Confirm the supervision level required for each procedure with your supervising dentist before performing it.

### Consequences of practicing outside scope

Practicing beyond the legal EFDA scope can result in loss of certification, disciplinary action and fines by the Florida Board of Dentistry, civil liability, and termination of employment. When in doubt about whether a task is within scope, ask your supervising dentist or contact the Board.

## Microbiology in the Dental Setting

Microorganisms are everywhere in the operatory — on instruments, surfaces, hands, the air, and inside patients' mouths. Most are harmless. A small fraction are pathogenic — capable of causing disease.

Four categories of microorganisms matter clinically:

- **Bacteria** — single-celled. Examples relevant to dentistry include *Streptococcus mutans* (caries), *Mycobacterium tuberculosis* (TB), and *Treponema pallidum* (syphilis).
- **Viruses** — submicroscopic, require a host cell to replicate. Critical examples: Hepatitis B (HBV), Hepatitis C (HCV), HIV, herpes simplex (HSV), and respiratory viruses.
- **Fungi** — examples include *Candida albicans* (oral thrush).
- **Prions** — abnormal proteins; rare but extraordinarily heat-resistant. Routine sterilization may not destroy them; prion-contaminated instruments are typically destroyed.

You don't need to memorize every pathogen. You do need to understand that infection control protocols are designed assuming the worst-case patient — because you cannot tell by looking which patient may be carrying a bloodborne pathogen.

## The Chain of Infection

For an infection to spread, six links must be present in sequence. Break any one and transmission stops.

1. **Infectious agent** — the pathogen itself (bacteria, virus, etc.)
2. **Reservoir** — where the pathogen lives and multiplies (the patient, contaminated water, an instrument surface)
3. **Portal of exit** — how the pathogen leaves the reservoir (saliva, blood, aerosol, lesion exudate)
4. **Mode of transmission** — direct contact, droplet, airborne, vehicle (instrument), or vector
5. **Portal of entry** — how it enters the next host (mucous membrane, non-intact skin, inhalation, ingestion)
6. **Susceptible host** — the next person, with compromised defenses or no immunity

In the dental setting, the strongest links to break are typically **mode of transmission** (sterilize instruments, disinfect surfaces, use PPE) and **portal of entry** (PPE again, hand hygiene, no contact with mucous membranes without barriers).

## Standard Precautions

The CDC and OSHA require dental settings to use **Standard Precautions** — the practice of treating every patient as if they could be infectious, regardless of known status. This replaces the older "Universal Precautions," which applied only to bloodborne pathogens; Standard Precautions extend the same protective approach to all body fluids, non-intact skin, and mucous membranes.

Key elements:

- Hand hygiene before and after every patient encounter
- PPE appropriate to the anticipated exposure
- Safe injection practices and sharps handling
- Respiratory hygiene and cough etiquette
- Sterilization of all critical and semi-critical instruments
- Environmental surface disinfection between patients

## Personal Protective Equipment (PPE)

PPE is your barrier against infectious exposure. The CDC and OSHA require specific PPE in the dental operatory:

### What to wear

- **Gloves** — exam gloves for all patient contact; nitrile preferred to reduce latex allergy risk; change between patients and any time integrity is compromised.
- **Mask** — fluid-resistant surgical mask covering nose and mouth; ASTM Level 1, 2, or 3 depending on procedure splatter/aerosol risk. Change between patients or when damp.
- **Protective eyewear** — wraparound side shields or full face shield; protects against splatter, aerosols, and instrument particles. Cleaned and disinfected between patients.
- **Protective clothing** — long-sleeved jacket or gown that closes high in the neck; covers exposed skin and street clothes; removed before leaving the clinical area and laundered (or disposed of) on-site.

### Donning sequence (putting on)

1. Hand hygiene
2. Gown
3. Mask
4. Eye protection
5. Gloves

### Doffing sequence (taking off)

1. Gloves
2. Gown
3. Eye protection
4. Mask
5. Hand hygiene

The sequence matters. Gloves come off first because they are the most contaminated. Mask comes off last because it has been protecting your breathing zone.

### Latex allergy

Latex allergy occurs in three forms — irritant dermatitis (skin redness, the most common but non-immunologic), Type IV delayed hypersensitivity (a contact dermatitis 6–48 hours after exposure), and Type I immediate hypersensitivity (potentially life-threatening, can include anaphylaxis). Use nitrile gloves to reduce risk for both staff and patients with known latex sensitivity.

## Hand Hygiene

Hand hygiene is the single most effective infection-control practice. Perform hand hygiene:

- Before and after every patient contact
- Before donning gloves and after removing them
- After contact with potentially contaminated surfaces
- Before leaving the operatory

### Two acceptable methods

- **Alcohol-based hand rub** — at least 60% alcohol; preferred when hands are not visibly soiled. Apply enough to cover all surfaces; rub until dry (~20 seconds).
- **Soap and water** — required when hands are visibly soiled. Wet, lather all surfaces including under fingernails and between fingers, scrub for at least 20 seconds, rinse, dry with single-use towel.

### Surgical hand antisepsis

For surgical procedures, a longer antiseptic hand scrub (3–6 minutes depending on product, per manufacturer instructions) is used.

## Cleaning, Disinfection, and Sterilization

These three terms are not interchangeable. They describe progressively more aggressive levels of microbial reduction.

| Process | Removes | Used For | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Cleaning** | Visible debris and most organic matter | Always the first step before disinfection or sterilization | Pre-soak in ultrasonic cleaner |
| **Disinfection** | Most pathogens (not all spores) | Non-critical and some semi-critical surfaces | Spray-wipe-spray with EPA-registered hospital disinfectant |
| **Sterilization** | All microorganisms including bacterial spores | Critical and semi-critical instruments | Steam autoclave at 121–134°C |

Cleaning must always precede disinfection or sterilization. Organic matter (blood, saliva, tissue) shields microorganisms from disinfectants and sterilants.

### Spaulding Classification (CDC)

Instruments are categorized by their use, which determines the required processing level:

- **Critical** — penetrate soft tissue or contact bone (e.g., surgical instruments, scalers, burs). Must be **sterilized**.
- **Semi-critical** — contact mucous membranes or non-intact skin but do not penetrate (e.g., mirrors, amalgam carriers). Must be **sterilized** if heat-tolerant; otherwise high-level disinfection.
- **Non-critical** — contact intact skin only (e.g., countertops, blood pressure cuffs). Cleaned and **disinfected** with low- to intermediate-level disinfectant.

## Sterilization Workflow

Every dental practice in Florida must follow this general workflow for critical and semi-critical instruments:

1. **Transport** — Used instruments transported to the sterilization area in a leak-proof, puncture-resistant container. Heavy utility gloves on the technician.
2. **Holding** — If processing is delayed, instruments are held in a moist (not dry) environment to prevent debris drying onto surfaces.
3. **Pre-cleaning** — Ultrasonic cleaner or instrument washer is used to remove visible debris. Hand-scrubbing is the last resort because of sharps injury risk.
4. **Rinsing and drying** — Rinse to remove cleaning solution; dry thoroughly to prevent moisture from interfering with sterilization.
5. **Inspection** — Check for damage, debris, integrity. Discard or repair as needed.
6. **Packaging** — Pouch or wrap each instrument set with the appropriate sterilization indicator (chemical indicator strip or tape). Date and identify the load.
7. **Sterilization** — Steam autoclave is the standard. Time and temperature depend on cycle type: typically 121°C for 30 minutes at 15 psi, or 134°C for 4 minutes at 30 psi for unwrapped instruments.
8. **Monitoring** — Three types of monitors confirm the cycle worked:
   - **Mechanical** — temperature, pressure, and time gauges on the autoclave
   - **Chemical** — indicator strips or tape that change color when exposed to sterilization conditions
   - **Biological (spore tests)** — at minimum weekly. *Geobacillus stearothermophilus* spore vials confirm the autoclave actually killed spores. The only definitive proof of sterilization.
9. **Storage** — Sterilized instruments stored in a clean, dry area separate from the dirty zone. Shelf life depends on packaging type and storage conditions; rotate stock.

### Clean zone vs. dirty zone

The sterilization room must have a clearly designated workflow direction: dirty instruments enter on one side, move through the processing steps, and emerge clean on the other side. Never reverse direction. Never store sterile instruments in the dirty zone.

## Regulatory Authorities

You need to know who regulates what — because if you miss a requirement, the agency responsible is who you'll hear from.

| Agency | Authority over |
|---|---|
| **Florida Board of Dentistry** | EFDA scope, supervision rules, license discipline |
| **CDC** (Centers for Disease Control) | Infection-control guidelines (recommendations, not enforceable directly) |
| **OSHA** (Occupational Safety & Health Admin) | Bloodborne Pathogens Standard, Hazard Communication, employee safety |
| **EPA** (Environmental Protection Agency) | Disinfectant registration, hazardous waste disposal, amalgam separator rule |
| **FDA** (Food and Drug Administration) | Medical device approval, sterilization equipment, sterilants |

When an OSHA inspector or Florida Board investigator visits your office, they will ask to see written policies, training records, and the office's exposure control plan. Familiarize yourself with where these documents are kept in your workplace.

## Chemical & Waste Management

Dental offices generate four types of waste, each handled differently:

- **General waste** — ordinary trash, no contamination concerns.
- **Contaminated waste** — items with blood or saliva that are not soaked or caked (e.g., gauze with small blood spotting). Goes in lined containers, regular pickup acceptable.
- **Regulated medical waste (biohazard)** — items saturated with blood, sharps, extracted teeth, pathology specimens. Red biohazard bags, sharps containers (puncture-resistant, leak-proof, labeled). Picked up by a licensed medical waste hauler.
- **Hazardous chemical waste** — fixer solution, lead foil from old film, mercury-contaminated amalgam waste. Subject to EPA disposal rules. Florida requires amalgam separators on vacuum systems since the EPA dental amalgam rule.

OSHA's Hazard Communication Standard requires Safety Data Sheets (SDS) for every chemical product used in the office. SDS must be accessible to all employees and contain chemical identity, hazards, first-aid measures, and disposal requirements.

## Dental Unit Waterlines

Dental unit waterlines (DUWLs) — the small tubing carrying water to handpieces, air-water syringes, and ultrasonic scalers — can harbor biofilm. Stagnant water in narrow tubes is ideal for bacterial colonization, particularly by *Legionella* species and other waterborne pathogens.

CDC standard for dental unit water quality: ≤ 500 CFU/mL (colony-forming units per milliliter) for non-surgical procedures.

### Maintenance

- Use independent water reservoirs separated from the municipal supply
- Flush waterlines for 20–30 seconds between patients
- Treat waterlines with EPA-registered chemical maintenance products per manufacturer instructions
- Test water quality periodically (commercial test kits available)
- For surgical procedures (involving bone or vessel exposure), use sterile irrigation, not waterline water

## A Brief Word on Dental Caries

Caries is included in this week's reading because it underpins the preventive procedures you'll learn in Week 4 (sealants, fluoride, polishing). Caries is a multifactorial bacterial disease in which acid-producing bacteria (primarily *Streptococcus mutans*) metabolize dietary sugars and produce acids that demineralize enamel and dentin.

Three conditions must coexist for caries to develop:

1. A susceptible tooth surface
2. Cariogenic bacteria
3. Fermentable substrate (sugars/carbs) over time

Preventive procedures (sealants, fluoride, oral hygiene education) interrupt one or more of these. You'll work with these interventions hands-on in Week 4.

## Key Takeaways

1. **You are an EFDA in Florida** — scope is defined by Chapter 466, F.S. and Rule 64B5, F.A.C. Practice within it; ask when uncertain.
2. **Standard Precautions treat every patient as potentially infectious.** It is not paranoia; it is the only rational protocol.
3. **PPE works only when used correctly.** Donning sequence matters. Doffing sequence matters more.
4. **Cleaning is not disinfection is not sterilization.** Always clean first.
5. **Spore tests are the only definitive proof.** Weekly minimum. Document every test.
6. **Five regulators matter:** Florida Board, CDC, OSHA, EPA, FDA. Know which is which.
7. **Waterlines harbor biofilm.** Maintain them or you are spraying contamination at every patient.
