## Three Layers Between the Pulp and the World

Restorative dentistry rebuilds tooth structure that's been lost — to caries, fracture, or wear. But you can't just pile material into a prepared cavity. The pulp underneath needs protection. The remaining dentin needs to bond reliably. The material needs to seal at the margins. Three EFDA-scope skills handle the in-between layer:

- **EFDA104** — apply liners, bases, and bonding agents in the correct sequence
- **EFDA105** — place temporary restorations when a final restoration isn't yet possible
- **EFDA108** — fabricate provisional crowns for prepped teeth awaiting their final crown

Get this layer right and the final restoration works. Get it wrong and the patient comes back in pain.

---

## Pulp Biology: Why the Sequence Matters

Underneath every restoration is a living pulp. The pulp houses odontoblasts, blood vessels, and nerves. Anything you put in the prepared cavity is sitting close — sometimes very close — to those structures.

Three pulp threats from restorative procedures:
- **Thermal** — drilling, exothermic cement setting reactions, hot/cold sensitivity through thin remaining dentin
- **Chemical** — acidic etchants, monomers in bonding resins, irritants in certain liners
- **Mechanical** — pulp exposure during preparation, micro-fracture from polymerization shrinkage

Liners and bases interpose between the cavity floor and the restorative material, blocking these threats. Bonding agents seal the tubules so saliva and bacteria can't infiltrate the dentin.

## EFDA104 · Liners, Bases, and Bonding Systems

### Liners

Liners are thin layers (less than 0.5 mm) applied directly to the cavity floor, especially over the deepest part of the preparation where dentin is thinnest. Two clinical purposes: protect the pulp and stimulate reparative dentin.

| Liner | Composition | Indication |
|---|---|---|
| **Calcium hydroxide** | Strong alkaline (pH ~12) | Deepest cavity floor, near or at pulp; stimulates reparative dentin |
| **Glass ionomer (GIC)** | Polyacrylic acid + ion-leachable glass | Releases fluoride; bonds chemically to dentin; thin liner under composite |
| **Resin-modified GIC** | GIC chemistry + photopolymer | Light-cured liner under composite; combines fluoride release with rapid set |

Calcium hydroxide is the classic "pulp cap" — placed in very deep preparations where the dentist judges the dentin is too thin to protect the pulp from thermal or chemical insult alone.

### Bases

Bases are thicker (usually 0.5–2 mm), placed over or in place of liners, to bulk up the cavity floor and provide thermal/mechanical insulation under the final restoration.

| Base | Use |
|---|---|
| **Zinc phosphate** | Classic; cementing crowns and bridges; under amalgam |
| **Zinc oxide eugenol (ZOE) — IRM** | Sedative effect on the pulp from eugenol; medium-strength base |
| **Glass ionomer / RMGI** | Modern preferred base under composite; bonds to dentin, releases fluoride |
| **Polycarboxylate** | Less common today; mild pulp response |

Important: eugenol-containing materials (ZOE, IRM) inhibit polymerization of resin composites. If a final composite restoration is planned, do NOT use a ZOE base. Use GIC or RMGI instead.

### Bonding systems

Modern composite restorations bond to enamel and dentin through a chemical-and-mechanical adhesion process. Three generations of bonding chemistry are in current use:

- **Etch-and-rinse (4th and 5th generation)** — phosphoric acid etch separately, rinse, then apply primer and bond. The classic "total etch" approach; strongest enamel bond.
- **Self-etch (6th and 7th generation)** — acidic primer simultaneously etches and conditions; no separate etch/rinse step. Faster, less technique-sensitive, slightly weaker enamel bond.
- **Universal adhesives (8th generation)** — can be used in either etch-and-rinse or self-etch mode; the dentist chooses based on the case.

### Layered sequence (composite restoration example)

1. Tooth prepared by dentist
2. Isolation (Week 2 territory: dam, cord, matrix)
3. **Liner** placed if very deep — calcium hydroxide or GIC at the deepest point only
4. **Base** placed if needed — RMGI to bulk up the floor
5. **Etch** the enamel and dentin (phosphoric acid, 15 seconds enamel + 10 seconds dentin)
6. **Rinse and lightly dry** — leave dentin moist, not desiccated
7. **Apply bonding agent** per manufacturer instructions; light-cure
8. **Place composite** in layered increments; cure each layer
9. **Adjust occlusion, contour, polish**

You assist the dentist through each step. Hand them the right material at the right moment, mix what needs mixing, cure when told.

## EFDA105 · Temporary Restorations

### When a tooth needs a "temp"

Temporary restorations cover a prepared tooth between visits when the final restoration isn't yet ready. Common reasons:
- Indirect restoration (crown, inlay, onlay) being fabricated in the lab
- Multi-visit endodontic treatment
- Emergency restoration when the patient must leave before the final can be placed
- Caries excavation in a tooth where the final restoration is scheduled later

A good temporary protects the pulp from thermal and chemical insult, prevents bacterial contamination of the prepared dentin, maintains tooth position (prevents drift), and provides the patient comfortable function.

### Common temporary materials

| Material | Best for |
|---|---|
| **IRM (Intermediate Restorative Material)** | Zinc oxide eugenol-based; classic short-term temp; sedative on pulp |
| **Cavit** | Pre-mixed, water-activated; quick endodontic access seal |
| **Zinc oxide non-eugenol (e.g., Coltosol)** | Use when final will be composite (avoids eugenol/resin inhibition) |
| **Glass ionomer / RMGI** | Longer-lived temporaries; fluoride release |
| **Resin temporary** | When esthetics matter (anterior teeth) |

### Placement principles

- Pack into the preparation to fill the cavity completely
- Adapt to the cavosurface margins
- Check occlusion — temp should not be high; high temps fracture or come loose
- Smooth the occlusal surface to prevent food impaction
- Document material used (the next clinician needs to know what's there)

## EFDA108 · Provisional Crowns

### Why a provisional crown matters

A provisional ("provis") is a temporary crown placed on a prepped tooth while the lab fabricates the final crown. The provisional has clinical duties:
- Protects the prepared tooth from sensitivity and bacterial invasion
- Maintains the tooth's position (no drift, no rotation)
- Maintains soft tissue contour (gingival health for the final cementation)
- Lets the patient eat and speak normally for 1–3 weeks

A poorly fitted provisional fails on all four counts and often comes off in the patient's hand at dinner.

### Two main techniques

**Preformed crown (shell technique)**

- Select a pre-made polycarbonate or stainless-steel crown shell sized to the tooth
- Adjust the shell with crown shears and bur to fit the prep margin and occlusion
- Reline the interior with provisional material (bis-acryl or methylmethacrylate)
- Cement with provisional cement

Best for: quick provisional in a single visit, especially for posterior teeth where esthetics are less critical.

**Direct method (matrix technique)**

- Take an alginate impression of the tooth BEFORE preparation
- After the dentist prepares the tooth, fill the impression matrix with provisional material
- Seat the matrix over the prepared tooth
- Allow material to set (usually 2–4 minutes)
- Remove the matrix; trim, contour, and finish the provisional
- Cement with provisional cement

Best for: anterior teeth (preserves original esthetics), or any case where pre-op anatomy needs to be replicated.

### Trimming and finishing

After placement, the provisional needs:
- Marginal trimming with a fine bur (no overhang)
- Occlusal adjustment — patient bites on articulating paper; high spots are removed
- Smooth, polished surface (rough surfaces irritate gingiva and accumulate plaque)
- Floss contacts to confirm clearance

### Provisional cement

Provisional cements are intentionally weak — designed to hold for 1–3 weeks but release easily when the lab crown arrives.

- **ZOE-based** — most common; sedative; do NOT use under a resin-bonded final (eugenol inhibits polymerization)
- **Non-eugenol** — when the final will be resin-bonded
- **Calcium hydroxide-based** — pulp-friendly; some pulp-capping cases

### Post-operative instructions for the patient

Teach the patient:
- Avoid sticky and chewy foods (gum, caramel) — they pull the provisional off
- Brush gently around the area; floss carefully (pull through, don't snap up between contacts)
- If the provisional comes off, save it, call the office, do not chew on the prepped tooth
- Some sensitivity to cold or pressure is normal for the first few days; severe or worsening pain is not — call the office

## Common Failures and How to Prevent Them

| Failure | Cause | Prevention |
|---|---|---|
| Provisional comes off in <48 hours | Loose fit, undercut not filled, cement too thin | Adapt margins better; check internal fit; mix cement to manufacturer ratio |
| Patient pain on bite | Occlusion too high | Check with articulating paper before dismissing patient |
| Open contact between prov and adjacent | Contact not closed during fabrication | Use direct method with intact pre-op impression |
| Final crown won't seat at delivery | Adjacent teeth drifted | Stable, well-fitted provisional from day one |
| Composite final won't bond | Eugenol residue from ZOE provisional cement | Use non-eugenol cement when composite final is planned |

## Key Takeaways

1. **Pulp protection is layered.** Deep liner (CaOH or GIC), then base (RMGI), then bond, then composite.
2. **Eugenol inhibits resin polymerization.** Never put ZOE under a composite final. Use GIC or RMGI.
3. **Dentin should be moist, not wet, not desiccated.** Bonding chemistry depends on it.
4. **A temporary restoration has four jobs:** pulp protection, bacterial seal, position maintenance, patient comfort.
5. **Provisional crowns are clinical, not cosmetic.** Marginal seal and occlusion matter even though it'll only last 1–3 weeks.
6. **Match the provisional cement to the planned final.** Non-eugenol if final is bonded; ZOE if final is conventional.
7. **Patient education is part of placement.** Teach what to eat, how to floss, when to call.
